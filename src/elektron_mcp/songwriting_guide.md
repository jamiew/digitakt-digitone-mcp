# Songwriting, composition, and beat-making guide

Use this guide for pop, rock, indie pop, indie rock, hip-hop, house, techno, disco, emotive diary-house, UK 140/dubstep, and drum & bass. Genre conventions are starting points. Every recommendation must explain the musical job it does, the cost of changing it, and how to test the result.

## The core system

A song is a controlled sequence of expectations. Give the listener something to recognize, a reason to keep listening, and a payoff or meaningful refusal of that payoff.

1. **Write the one-sentence brief.** Name the listener, setting, emotion, physical energy, and central image. Example: “A private voice note turns into a shared 2 a.m. release without losing its sadness.”
2. **Name the identity.** Pick the groove, hook, riff, sample, lyric, progression, or sound that must survive every section. If none is memorable after silence, keep writing before arranging.
3. **Assign roles.** Foundation, pulse, bass, harmony, foreground voice, response, texture, and transition. One sound may fill two roles. Two busy foreground voices usually compete.
4. **Build one complete cell.** Make the fullest useful four-, eight-, or sixteen-bar version. Include a phrase turn, not only a loop.
5. **Map functions before details.** Decide what each section must do: establish, develop, withhold, lift, release, destabilize, recover, or close.
6. **Prototype subtractively.** Copy the full cell across a rough timeline and remove parts. Add new material only when subtraction cannot create the needed contrast.
7. **Make boundaries audible.** Use at least two independent cues when clarity matters: fill, silence, pickup, lyric turn, harmonic arrival, register shift, density change, new timbre, or automation move.
8. **Audition the joins.** Hear at least one phrase before and after every boundary. Judge the landing, not the fill alone.
9. **Run the identity test.** Mute the signature part. If the song becomes generic, the identity matters. If nothing changes, choose a stronger identity or make its treatment more specific.
10. **Print a full draft early.** A flawed three-minute song teaches more about form than a polished eight-bar loop.

Keep a short song card:

- **Promise:** what the listener should feel or understand.
- **Identity:** the part that makes this song itself.
- **Hook:** the shortest repeatable musical, lyrical, rhythmic, or sonic idea.
- **Arc:** where tension rises, releases, and changes meaning.
- **Rules:** two conventions to keep.
- **Risk:** one convention to test breaking.
- **Open question:** the next decision that blocks completion.

Preserve rejected lyric drafts and a raw source pattern. Reversibility keeps experiments cheap.

## Applied music theory

Theory names repeatable effects. It does not grant permission. Start with the sound and function, then use the name to reason about alternatives.

### Rhythm and groove

- **Pulse** is the beat a body follows. **Meter** groups pulses. **Subdivision** divides them. A pattern can imply half-time or double-time without changing the audio tempo.
- **Anchors** make the grid legible: a four-on-the-floor kick, backbeat, clave, ride pulse, repeated bass attack, or lyric stress. **Syncopation** gains force by leaning against an anchor. If every part avoids the grid, nothing feels displaced.
- Build nested cycles. A one-bar drum anchor, two-bar bass answer, four-bar melodic phrase, eight-bar variation, and sixteen-bar transition create long form without long individual patterns.
- Velocity is hierarchy, not randomization. Choose primary, secondary, ghost, and accidental-looking hits. Human feel comes from consistent tendencies: a laid-back snare, rushed shaker, shortened open hat, or changing articulation.
- Move timing after the pattern works on-grid. Microtiming cannot rescue weak accent design. Compare the quantized and moved versions at equal loudness.
- Kick and bass need a deliberate relationship: unison for weight, alternating attacks for bounce, or selective overlap with controlled duration. “Sidechain it” is not a writing decision.
- Fills should redirect attention. A fill can increase activity, remove the downbeat, change pitch, or create silence. The next downbeat carries the payoff.

A groove audit asks:

1. Can kick, backbeat, bass, and pulse explain the feel alone?
2. Which event is the strongest in each bar, and was that intentional?
3. Does each syncopation answer a stable reference?
4. Do bass and lead converse, or speak continuously together?
5. Does the final bar turn toward the next phrase?

### Melody, motifs, and hooks

- Start with a two- to five-note motif or a distinctive rhythm. Repeat it exactly once, then vary one dimension: ending, interval, rhythm, register, harmony, or timbre.
- Contour is the melody’s shape. Stepwise motion reads as connected; leaps attract attention. A leap often feels balanced by stepwise motion in the other direction, but breaking that balance can create insistence or instability.
- Give an important phrase a clear peak, low point, or withheld note. A chorus can lift through register, but it can also feel larger by becoming simpler, lower, wider, or more rhythmically direct.
- Land important syllables on intentional notes. Roots feel grounded; thirds state chord quality; fifths feel open; sevenths and ninths add color or suspension.
- Separate hook identity from section length. A hook is usually a short lyrical, melodic, rhythmic, riff, sample, or sound-design kernel inside a longer section.
- Singability is a choice, not a universal goal. For a communal hook, use a compact range, repeatable rhythm, and open vowels. For an anxious or intimate line, a narrow repeated pitch, interrupted breath, or unresolved ending may work better.
- In rap, pitch may be secondary, but cadence still has contour, motif, register, tension, and release. Treat rhyme placement and vowel color as arrangement events.

Hook test: play or sing the idea once, wait ten seconds, then reproduce it without the session. If only the sound preset is memorable, the writing may still need an identity.

### Harmony and bass

- Establish a tonal center even when no functional cadence exists. Pedal tones, repeating bass notes, drones, and sample roots can create center without classical dominant-to-tonic motion.
- Harmonic rhythm matters as much as chord choice. One chord per bar, two beats, phrase, or whole section creates different motion with the same chord set.
- Common loops are useful because the listener understands them quickly. Examples include `I–V–vi–IV`, `vi–IV–I–V`, `i–♭VI–♭III–♭VII`, `I–♭VII–IV`, two-chord modal vamps, and one-chord grooves. Use scale degrees so the idea survives transposition.
- Voice leading makes simple harmony feel authored. Hold common tones, move other notes by step, control the top note as a melody, and let the bass choose inversion and weight.
- Borrowed chords change color quickly. A minor `iv` in major, major `IV` in minor, `♭VII`, or chromatic bass approach works best when a common tone or melodic target explains the move.
- In sample-based hip-hop, harmony may already be dense. A new bass note can clarify, contradict, or reframe the sample. Check it against the sample’s lowest stable pitch instead of assuming the detected key is right.
- House and disco often get harmonic motion from voicing, extensions, bass rhythm, and filter movement over a short vamp. Techno, 140, and drum & bass can use a pedal center so rhythm and timbre carry the form.
- Harmonic complexity is not emotional complexity. A one-chord track can tell a rich story through orchestration and timing. A six-chord loop can feel static if every section repeats it identically.

When harmony feels wrong, test in this order: bass note, melody collision, voicing/register, chord duration, then chord identity.

### Lyrics, topline, and prosody

- State the song’s premise in one plain sentence. The title or central phrase should sharpen that premise, not merely name the topic.
- Verses usually add evidence, time, place, action, or contradiction. Choruses usually compress the emotional claim. Pre-choruses redirect momentum. Bridges reveal a new angle, consequence, or decision.
- Use concrete nouns and verbs before abstract explanation. One specific room, object, gesture, or message can carry more emotion than several adjectives.
- Prosody aligns natural speech stress, rhythmic accent, melodic emphasis, harmony, texture, and meaning. If the melody emphasizes an unimportant syllable, make that mismatch purposeful or rewrite it.
- Rhyme creates expectation. Exact rhyme closes; slant rhyme loosens; delayed rhyme holds tension. Internal rhyme can drive rap flow without forcing every line ending.
- Repeat the hook enough to become memory, then change context around it. A final chorus can gain meaning through a new preceding lyric, a changed bass note, missing drums, an added response, or one altered word.
- A sampled voice is a person and a narrative source, not only texture. Preserve context, seek permission where needed, and decide whether chopping clarifies or distorts the speaker’s meaning.

## Form and arrangement

Name sections by function before naming them verse, chorus, drop, or breakdown.

- **Establish:** teach the identity and pulse.
- **Develop:** repeat with meaningful change.
- **Withhold:** remove an expected anchor or delay a payoff.
- **Lift:** increase perceived possibility through register, harmony, rhythm, density, or lyric direction.
- **Release:** deliver the promised hook, groove, or downbeat.
- **Destabilize:** introduce a new key, texture, meter, sample, silence, or perspective.
- **Recover:** make an earlier idea return with changed meaning.
- **Close:** reduce future expectation through cadence, repetition, thinning, cutoff, or fade.

A section boundary can change one or more axes:

| Axis | Lower or narrower | Higher or wider |
|---|---|---|
| Density | fewer active roles | more active roles |
| Register | compact or low | wide or high |
| Rhythm | sustained or sparse | subdivided or syncopated |
| Harmony | pedal or stable loop | faster changes or chromatic color |
| Timbre | dark, dry, narrow | bright, wet, wide |
| Foreground | instrumental or absent | vocal, lead, riff, or sample |
| Space | continuous | stops, tails, throws, and gaps |
| Text | detail and setup | title, claim, consequence, or reversal |

Change the smallest set of axes that makes the new function clear. If every axis changes at once, save that scale of contrast for the moment that deserves it.

### Transitions

- **Preparation:** riser, fill, dominant, pickup, accelerating rhythm, narrowing filter, or lyric cue predicts the boundary.
- **Pivot:** one part belongs to both sections, such as a held vocal, common chord, drum loop, or bass note.
- **Hard cut:** silence or immediate replacement makes contrast itself the event.
- **Fuzzy boundary:** some tracks anticipate the boundary while others continue through it. This avoids a vertical “all clips change here” seam.
- **False arrival:** the expected downbeat is withheld, reduced, or delayed. Use sparingly and keep another cue so the listener knows the miss was intentional.

Every temporary automation move needs a start and return value. Otherwise the next section inherits an accident.

## Genre playbooks

Bar counts below are defaults for sketching, not compliance tests. The right length is the shortest version that completes the section’s job.

### Pop, rock, indie pop, and indie rock

**Center of gravity:** a vocal, title, riff, or emotional turn.

- Make verse and chorus functions identifiable even when they share chords. Change melodic rhythm, register, lyric density, drum behavior, guitar articulation, vocal layers, or width.
- A common scaffold is intro → verse → pre-chorus → chorus → verse → pre-chorus → chorus → bridge or instrumental turn → final chorus → outro. Strophic, AABA, through-composed, and refrain forms remain valid.
- The verse earns attention with detail and motion. The chorus frames the central idea more clearly. A pre-chorus should redirect energy; remove it if it only delays the chorus.
- Rock contrast often lives in performance: cymbal state, guitar voicing, articulation, dynamics, doubling, and whether the riff or singer owns the foreground.
- Indie writing can keep asymmetry, dry intimacy, odd harmony, loose forms, or deliberately small choruses. Preserve one recurring cue so “unconventional” does not become “unremembered.”
- For a final lift, first try changed context rather than automatic key change: new counterline, octave, backing voice, missing first downbeat, or reharmonized final hook.

**Failure mode:** every section uses the same vocal register, drum pattern, guitar texture, and chord voicing. Fix one contrast axis before adding more tracks.

### Hip-hop

**Center of gravity:** the rapper’s cadence plus one unmistakable beat identity.

- Treat the rapper as the lead before a vocal exists. Leave rhythmic and spectral gaps where phrases, breaths, ad-libs, and line endings can land.
- Eight- and sixteen-bar units are useful writing grids, not laws. Count the actual cadence. A hook may be four or eight bars; a verse may be twelve, sixteen, twenty-four, or shaped around the lyric.
- Use strategic drops, bass changes, sample filters, drum substitutions, turnarounds, and response phrases to mark form without replacing the whole beat.
- A strong sample loop can remain almost unchanged if vocal narrative and small phrase turns create motion. Variation should support the verse, not demand attention from it.
- Beat switches work when they change the lyric’s perspective, energy, or speaker. Preserve a shared sample, tempo, key center, texture, or transition cue if continuity matters.
- Boom bap often benefits from deliberate kick variation, a legible backbeat, and human sample timing. Trap and newer forms may use faster hat subdivisions and sparse harmonic rhythm, but the same foreground rule applies.

**Failure mode:** the instrumental is a finished solo track with no room for a voice. Mute the busiest lead and rap nonsense syllables over the beat; keep the space that improves the flow.

### House and disco

**Center of gravity:** bodily groove, kick-bass relationship, and a compact riff, vocal, chord stab, or guitar figure.

- Four-on-the-floor kick and backbeats on 2 and 4 provide a common house grid. Offbeat hats, shuffled sixteenths, percussion, and syncopated bass create motion around it.
- Build in 4-, 8-, 16-, and 32-bar layers because dancers and DJs hear phrase-scale arrivals. Do not force every change onto the same cycle.
- Disco and funk thrive on interlocking short parts. Guitar, bass, keys, percussion, strings, and vocals should leave holes for one another. Economy and articulation beat constant layering.
- House sections often change by addition, subtraction, filter range, send level, bass access, and vocal exposure rather than new chords.
- A DJ-oriented version may need mixable drum-led entrances and exits. A song-oriented or streaming edit may start with the hook and omit them. Choose the use case instead of splitting the difference.
- Breakdowns expose the construction. Drops restore the foundation with at least one new fact: changed bass answer, vocal context, ride, chord inversion, percussion, or phrase ending.

**Failure mode:** adding one part every eight bars creates a predictable staircase. Sometimes add two related parts, remove one at the same moment, or let a part cross the boundary early or late.

### Techno

**Center of gravity:** pulse, sound character, and controlled change over time.

- Choose the mood before the form. Hypnotic tracks favor slow morphing and nested variation. Rave-oriented tracks tolerate abrupt short contrasts. Driving tracks hold energy while rotating color. Minimal tracks make tiny events carry narrative weight.
- Change one main dimension at a time: density, brightness, register, space, rhythmic subdivision, distortion, or modulation depth. This makes the cause of the energy change audible.
- Repetition is not a defect when the repeated object rewards attention. Use evolving timbre, polymetric loop lengths, conditional events, accents, and occasional negative space.
- A build can shorten event spacing, raise spectral energy, narrow the stereo image before release, or remove low-end certainty. A breakdown need not become ambient; it only needs to alter the anchor.
- Long-form club function may justify several minutes with few harmonic changes. The arrangement must still establish, develop, and close expectations.

**Failure mode:** constant “ear candy” destroys hypnosis. Remove decorative events until each remaining change has consequence.

### Emotive diary-house and electronic pop

This lens covers the intimate-to-communal approach associated with voice-note collage, warm piano or synth harmony, and club rhythm. Do not copy a particular artist’s surface sound; copy the problem-solving method.

**Center of gravity:** a human document with emotional context.

- Begin with a voice memo, field recording, phone video, or spoken phrase whose cadence and meaning suggest pitch and rhythm.
- Find the smallest emotionally complete phrase. Keep enough surrounding breath, noise, or speech to preserve humanity.
- Let harmony answer the speaker before drums arrive. Then let the dance pulse change the scale of the feeling from private to shared.
- Reintroduce the sample with changed chopping, harmony, register, crowd-like response, or silence so repetition advances the story.
- Imperfect recordings, room noise, and abrupt edits can be identity. Clean only what blocks meaning or groove.
- Consent and context are compositional constraints. If manipulation changes a person’s meaning, stop or make the transformation explicit.

A useful arc is document → recognition → pulse → collective release → exposed return. Break it when the source material asks for another story.

**Failure mode:** a random quote sits over generic house. The spoken cadence, harmony, and form should grow from the same emotional phrase.

### UK 140 and dubstep

**Center of gravity:** sub-bass weight, negative space, and call-and-response sound design.

- Work around 140 BPM with a half-time body feel when that serves the style. A common landmark is the main snare or clap on beat 3, but two-step and broken variants need not obey one grid.
- Eight-bar blocks are a useful sketch: intro, build, drop A, drop B, drop C, reset, second drop, outro. Within a drop, use two- or four-bar calls and answers.
- Keep sub and mid-bass roles clear. The sub carries pitch and physical weight; mid layers carry articulation and character. Silence between bass statements is part of the riff.
- Shuffled percussion, hats, ghosts, and syncopated kicks create forward motion around the half-time landmark.
- A drop needs an identifiable bass or sample phrase, not continuous maximum density. Change the answer, tail, register, or modulation before inventing a new patch.
- Builds can reduce low end, narrow rhythm, or expose a vocal. The drop’s first bar should prove why the build existed.

**Failure mode:** every bass sound speaks at once. Reduce the drop to sub, landmark snare, and one call; add only answers that improve the sentence.

### Drum & bass

**Center of gravity:** fast break grammar, bass motion, and long phrase control.

- Most modern drum & bass is counted around 170–176 BPM, often 174. Software may report half-time, double-time, or an unrelated pulse. Validate the grid against repeated snares and phrase boundaries.
- A common two-step skeleton places strong snares on beats 2 and 4, with kicks and ghosted break events creating syncopation. Amen-derived, liquid, jungle, neuro, halftime, and footwork-influenced tracks alter that grammar.
- Build 16- and 32-bar forms from shorter break, bass, pad, vocal, and fill cycles. Change break edits at phrase turns without erasing the dancer’s reference.
- Keep sub phrases readable under busy drums. Let bass sustain when the break chatters, or let the break simplify when bass becomes conversational.
- Intro and breakdown establish atmosphere and mixability; drops establish the primary break-bass contract; second drops should change the contract, not merely increase loudness.
- Jungle can foreground the break as the lead voice. Liquid forms often foreground harmony or vocal. Neuro forms may foreground bass timbre. Choose one.

**Failure mode:** the fast grid makes every track continuously active. Preserve holes at several scales: between hits, between bass statements, and between sections.

### Weaving genres without making a collage

Choose one answer on each axis:

- **Structural home:** pop song, rap song, DJ tool, long-form techno, or drop-driven bass track.
- **Groove home:** rock backbeat, hip-hop pocket, house pulse, 140 half-time, or drum & bass two-step.
- **Emotional home:** lyric narrative, bodily repetition, sample memory, riff aggression, or timbral hypnosis.
- **Borrowed vocabulary:** one or two outside techniques, such as indie guitar over DnB, hip-hop sample grammar over house, or diary vocals over techno.

Hybridize one axis at a time. A pop arc can hold DnB drums and indie guitars. A house structure can hold hip-hop chops and diary vocals. If every section changes all four axes, use a shared motif, voice, bass center, or transition sound as glue.

## Beat-making on pattern machines

A pattern is stored machine state. A phrase is a musical thought. A section gives phrases a job. A song row schedules that job.

- Keep a source pattern with the fullest useful cell.
- Derive intro, verse, hook, breakdown, build, and drop patterns by subtraction first.
- Use conditional trigs for local variation whose exact occurrence may drift.
- Use a separate pattern or song row when a transition must happen once, several tracks must change together, the row length changes, or automation must reset precisely.
- Offset nested cycles when the machine allows it: one-bar kick, two-bar percussion, four-bar bass, eight-bar lead turn. Avoid making every track restart together unless rigidity is the point.
- Reserve at least one track or sound role for transitions, resampling, or a foreground response. If all tracks are full, the arrangement has no breathing room.
- On Digitakt, notes 0–7 trigger audio tracks 1–8. Use track roles consistently enough to edit quickly, but let a section repurpose a track when the musical function is clear.
- On Digitone, protect the shared eight-voice budget. Long releases, dense chords, and overlapping melodies can steal voices. Arrangement includes deciding which notes may end.
- Reset temporary filter, resonance, envelope, volume, pan, LFO, delay, and reverb changes at the intended boundary.

A useful pattern family is `SOURCE`, `LOW`, `A`, `A'`, `B`, `TURN`, and `OUT`. Fewer well-related patterns beat many near-duplicates.

## Studying reference songs with Cipher

Reference analysis is not template theft. Ask one question at a time: How early is the identity stated? What changes at the first payoff? How long can the groove remain stable? How does the second drop differ? Where does the vocal disappear?

Cipher provides evidence, not semantic truth:

- `analysis.json` contains duration, estimated BPM, key, confidence, and counts.
- `beats.txt` and `beats_raw.txt` provide beat candidates.
- `ws_cache.json` contains eight chroma-derived section candidates, waveform peaks, stem peaks, and stem loudness.
- `chords.json` contains major/minor triad estimates.
- `lyrics.json`, stem transcripts, stems, and MIDI help locate vocal, pitch, and orchestration changes.

Current implementation limits matter:

- Tempo comes from Librosa beat tracking. Half-time, double-time, and competing pulses are normal errors.
- Key uses Krumhansl–Schmuckler profile correlation. Confidence compares profile fit; it is not certainty.
- Chords are frame-wise matches against only 24 major/minor triads. Extensions, inversions, power chords, samples, and noisy spectra can produce flicker or false harmony.
- Sections are exactly eight agglomerative chroma segments, nudged about 100 ms earlier. Labels `A` through `H` are chronological, not repeated-form or verse/chorus labels. Timbre-only and drum-only changes may be missed; short chromatic events may be over-segmented.
- Stem separation leaks. Treat stems within roughly 20 dB of the loudest as potentially present, then listen.

### Cipher reference workflow

1. Write an ear/public hypothesis before opening the analysis: likely BPM family, section functions, hook, groove anchor, and expected form.
2. Read `analysis.json`. Normalize plausible half/double tempo before converting seconds to bars.
3. Read `ws_cache.json`. Treat each boundary as a candidate. Snap it to the nearest convincing beat or lyric event by listening.
4. Inspect stem activity, lyrics, and the lead sheet. Rename sections by function. Repeated functions may receive the same name even when Cipher assigns different letters.
5. Build a table with start time, bar, length, function, active roles, foreground, harmony, energy, transition cues, and the new fact introduced.
6. Compare the table with the relevant genre default. Mark each convention as kept, varied, or broken.
7. For each break, identify what replaced the convention’s job. If nothing replaced it, decide whether productive ambiguity or an unfinished transition explains the result.
8. Borrow the structural relationship, not the copyrighted content: “voice disappears for eight bars before the second payoff,” not the melody or lyric.

### What the current Cipher corpus teaches

These are machine observations from the local Cipher analyses, not canonical song-form claims.

| Reference | Cipher observation | Practical lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Lorde, “Million Dollar Bills” | 107.7 BPM; the fixed eight-way split includes one long ~21-bar span and several one- to three-bar slices | Pop function cannot be inferred from chroma cuts alone. Use lyric recurrence, vocal contour, and production changes to name verse and hook. |
| Mitski, “Washing Machine Heart” | 107.7 BPM; a ~27-bar region and ~14-bar region dominate, followed by short fragments | A concise indie-pop song can use uneven machine-visible regions while recurring vocal and groove cues hold identity. |
| A Tribe Called Quest, “Can I Kick It?” | 95.7 BPM; several candidates land near eight bars and two near twenty-eight bars | Long rap spans and short returning hook-like spans can coexist over a stable sample. Chord labels on sample-rich audio need skepticism. |
| Jamie xx and Honey Dijon, “Baddy on the Floor” | 123 BPM; Cipher finds useful ~8- and ~23-bar regions but also one ~56-bar region | House can change through layers, vocals, and timbre while chroma stays similar. A harmony-led detector under-segments arrangement. |
| Daft Punk, “Around the World” | 123 BPM; long ~46-, ~23-, and ~26-bar candidates dominate | Repetitive harmony can support long form when orchestration and groove combinations carry the narrative. |
| Nia Archives, “Sober Feels” | Cipher reports 112.3 BPM and mixed five-, sixteen-, and thirty-three-bar candidates | For drum & bass, validate tempo and bars against the break. A plausible-looking BPM can be the wrong perceptual grid. |
| Led Zeppelin, “Whole Lotta Love” | 172.3 BPM; very long regions surround short ~4-, ~5-, and ~10-bar events | Rock form can make radical breakdowns and short transitional events the point. Uneven length works when riff and performance restore continuity. |

The corpus contradicts any universal “change every eight bars” rule. It supports a better rule: make the listener’s model change often enough for the style, and keep enough continuity to understand the change.

## When to break the rules

Break a convention when its normal effect conflicts with the song’s goal, not because convention feels uncool.

Use the **job–cost–replacement–test** method:

1. **Name the convention.** Example: “The chorus arrives after the first verse.”
2. **Name its job.** Early identity, memory, payoff, dance orientation, singability, or mixability.
3. **Name the desired violation.** Delay the chorus to create longing.
4. **Name the cost.** The listener may not know what to remember.
5. **Add a replacement cue.** Tease the title rhythm, chord, sample, or contour earlier.
6. **Commit clearly.** A decisive odd phrase sounds intentional; an almost-normal one can sound mistaken.
7. **Test both versions in context.** Keep the break only if it strengthens the brief.

| Default | Job | Productive break | Replacement cue or test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Four-, eight-, or sixteen-bar phrase | prediction and group movement | truncate or extend for lyric, riff, or tension | keep a pickup, pedal, backbeat, or repeated motif; compare the landing |
| Chorus after verse | early hook and orientation | delay, omit, or reveal it once | recurring refrain, riff, title rhythm, or escalating verse ending |
| Chorus is bigger | clear payoff | make it intimate, empty, low, or rhythmically plain | stronger lyric claim, harmonic arrival, or sudden focus |
| Backbeat or four-on-floor stays fixed | bodily reference | remove, displace, or mutate it | another pulse, bass attack, vocal cadence, or brief duration preserves orientation |
| Chords stay in key | tonal coherence | borrowed chord, chromatic bass, or abrupt key field | common tone, melodic target, voice leading, or narrative reason |
| Drop restores everything | release | false drop, partial return, or no drop | preserve one unmistakable arrival and make withholding serve the arc |
| Every section is distinct | easy form recognition | blur boundaries or continuously morph | stagger entries and exits while one identity remains stable |
| DJ intro/outro | mixability | hook-first song edit | choose listening context; do not make a compromise that serves neither |
| One genre grammar | stylistic clarity | hybrid groove, structure, and sound | choose structural and emotional homes; borrow only one or two outside vocabularies |
| Clean source audio | polish | phone noise, clipping, room sound, rough edit | confirm the artifact carries identity or truth rather than distraction |

A rule break is weak when it has no audible purpose, when several unrelated rules break at once, or when explanation is more interesting than the result.

## Diagnosis and revision

### “The loop is good, but it is not a song”

- Duplicate the full cell into a rough timeline.
- Make `LOW`, `A`, `A'`, `B`, `TURN`, and `OUT` versions by muting or deleting.
- Write one genuinely contrasting phrase with the same sound palette, or keep the sequence and change one sound family.
- Offset loop lengths so all parts do not restart together.
- Print a draft before improving transitions.

### “The chorus or drop feels weak”

Check in order:

1. Was the payoff promised or foreshadowed?
2. Did the preceding section leave room to grow?
3. Did the hook become clearer?
4. Did the low end, backbeat, register, or lyric actually arrive?
5. Did too many transition effects steal the impact?

Try removal before addition. A half-beat of silence or a stripped first bar can feel larger than another layer.

### “The beat feels stiff”

- Establish primary and ghost velocities.
- Shorten or lengthen articulations.
- Move one family, not every hit.
- Let hats, percussion, and bass have different cycles.
- Compare on-grid and moved versions at equal loudness.

### “The arrangement is busy”

Name the foreground in each section. Mute every part that duplicates its rhythm, register, or message without improving it. If two leads must coexist, make one sustain while the other moves, or separate their calls.

### “The melody wanders”

Reduce it to its most recognizable rhythm and two to five pitches. Pick one peak or target note. Repeat the opening, then change only the ending. Test it over a drone before blaming the chords.

### “The harmony is generic”

Keep the chords and change harmonic rhythm, bass inversion, top voice, register, articulation, or one borrowed color. Generic voicing is more common than generic scale degrees.

### “The transition sounds pasted on”

Use material from the song: reverse the hook tail, extend a lyric breath, turn the bass ending into a pickup, or let one track cross the boundary. Generic risers should support, not replace, musical cause.

## Assistant output contract

When applying this guide:

- Start with the diagnosis and the song’s likely identity.
- For a hybrid, explicitly name the structural home, groove home, emotional home, and borrowed vocabulary before planning sections.
- Distinguish observation from inference, especially with Cipher output.
- Give two to four options only when they create meaningfully different effects; name the tradeoff.
- Use exact bars, beats, roles, notes, scale degrees, mutes, or automation targets. Avoid “add variation” without saying what changes and why.
- Keep one variable per comparison. Preserve a raw pattern or draft.
- Include one “when to break it” option and the cue that replaces the broken convention’s job.
- End with the smallest useful listening test or next action.
